2 · Make it good on your rooms

Test photos are not your properties. This is where quality is actually won or lost, and it cannot start without your photographs.

  • Run every photo you send, the day it arrives
  • Tune the model settings for your room types and lighting
  • Fix the specific failures we find — wrong counts, drifting proportions, style misses
  • Agree what "good enough to show a customer" means

What we need from you

  • 10–15 photographs of real rooms — the kind you actually sell into, not staged shots. Wide enough to show a door or a window.
  • Two or three examples of designs you consider good, so we are aiming at your taste rather than mine
  • One hour together to review the first batch

3 · Attach your products

The render shows a look. Beside it, a list of your actual products that fit that room — with prices and, crucially, whether they physically fit.

  • Load your catalogue and match products to what the system finds in each room
  • Check sizes against the space — no recommending a 240cm sofa for a 200cm wall
  • Show products beside each design, with price and a link

What we need from you

  • Your catalogue as a spreadsheet. One row per product: code, name, category, width / depth / height in cm, price, and a photo filename.
  • 30–50 products across five or six categories is enough to start
  • The product photos themselves

4 · Put it in front of customers

A small, controlled pilot. Real customers, watched closely, before anything is announced publicly.

  • Add whatever a real customer needs that a demo does not — saving, sharing, downloading
  • Run with a handful of customers and watch what actually happens
  • Review twice a week — what they asked for, what confused them

What we need from you

  • One person on your side who fields their questions
  • A decision on who pays for what if this becomes a paid service

Pilot customers identified by Fri 18 Sep

Decisions we need from you

These change what gets built, so the earlier they are settled the better.

Which rooms matter most? Bedrooms, living rooms, whole flats? One room type done properly beats four done badly. This decides where the tuning effort goes.
Who is this for — your team, or your customers? A tool your designers use internally is a different product from one a customer uses alone on their phone. It affects the interface, the hosting and the cost.
Does it need to show your products, or just the look? If products matter, phase 3 is essential and the catalogue is on the critical path. If inspiration is enough, we can skip it and move starts a week earlier.
Should customers be able to save and come back? That means accounts and a database — about a week of extra work. Worth it for a real product, unnecessary for a pilot.

Worth saying plainly

  • It will not draw your exact sofa. No image generator can do that today — not this one, not ChatGPT. The picture sells the look; the product list beside it sells the product.
  • Quality on your rooms is genuinely unknown until phase 2. That is why phase 2 exists, and why the photographs are the most useful thing you can send.
  • This is a fast plan and it is tight. It works because the build work is small and mostly done. It stops working the moment material is late — the two dates at the top are the whole schedule.
  • What it will not do is check building regulations, produce working drawings, or replace a designer's judgement. It gets the conversation started faster.
